*What was built:*

- **A white balance** (`Vista.Core/Output/DisplayGrade.cs`, `--white-balance none | <kelvin>K`, sheet axis `whiteBalance`): a camera's
  Kelvin setting. A Planckian light of that temperature (its chromaticity from Kim et al.'s cubic fit of the locus, 1667–25 000 K) is
  adapted to the display's white by Bradford's transform, in XYZ between the engine's own Rec.709 matrices, so the display's white is
  exactly the white the engine maps to (1, 1, 1) and a white keeps its luminance. It is applied to the PNG's linear colour before the
  exposure; the EXR stays scene-referred and unbalanced, as a raw file is. `none` (the default) is today's: the display's own D65.
- **The punchy look's margin** (`--sky-margin <share>`, sheet axis `skyMargin`): M1.11c holds the look's saturation back just enough
  that no Rec.709 channel goes below 0; a margin holds it back until every channel is at least that share of the colour's luminance,
  never below the look's power alone (saturation 1). 0 (the default) is M1.11c's bytes.
- **The exposure at dusk** (`--adaptation 0..1`, sheet axis `adaptation`; `IncidentMeter.AdaptationEv`): how far the incident meter
  follows the light below `IncidentMeter.AdaptationLux`, 25 000 lux on the card (the sun about 2° up; the hero's golden hour reads
  28 000). 1 (the default) follows it all the way, M1.10a's meter; 0 holds the exposure there, as a camera set before dusk; between
  follows that share of the drop in stops. Above the threshold nothing changes, so golden hour is the meter's whatever the setting. The
  average meter ignores it. Sidecar schema 27 records all three (`image.whiteBalanceKelvin`, `image.skyMargin`, `exposure.adaptation`,
  `exposure.adaptationEv`).
- **The golden suite's tools** (stop 2 takes the goldens): `vista-views/goldens/suite.json` lists ten previews at seed 42 and 64 spp,
  each named with the M1 check it keeps: the sky, the aerial and default views, the down-slope view's pools, the hero, alpenglow at
  dusk and at dawn, April's bare steep faces, valley mist, deep twilight. `Vista.App --golden-check [--suite …] [--only a,b] [--update]
  [--out folder]` (`Vista.App/Gpu/GoldenCheck.cs`) renders them through one session as `--preview` would and compares each PNG and EXR
  with the golden beside the suite (`Vista.Core/Output/GoldenComparison.cs`): identical, within the tolerance, different or missing;
  `--update` re-baselines, deliberately, and says what each moved by. The tolerance (the suite's own, `GoldenTolerance`): the EXR's mean
  luminance within 1%, its 16-pixel blocks' luminance within 3% rms of the mean, and each of the PNG's channel means within 1 level. Not
  pixel by pixel: a driver that rounds one operation differently sends a path another way and re-rolls its pixel's noise, which averages
  out in blocks; per channel, so a change of colour at the same luminance shows. `Vista.Core/Output/ExrDecoder.cs` reads back the EXRs
  `ExrEncoder` writes (single-part scanline, half or float, none/ZIPS/ZIP).
- **The air's tables in fewer dispatches** (`D3D12VistaRenderer.Atmosphere.cs`, `LutChunk`): on a shared GPU each LUT builder's
  dispatches are sized from its own measured rate to about 20 ms (two thirds of the cap's 30), never under 65 536 items, instead of a
  fixed quarter of a million items (2–8 ms each). Every texel is computed alone, so no table changes.
- **The sign-off's sheets** (`vista-views/signoff/render.ps1`): each view rendered once and displayed under every candidate (display
  options re-expose a render; `--sheet` never traces it again). `signoff/hero.camera.json` is the auto camera's pick on M1's hero scene
  (`autocam/range-golden.recipe.json`, seed 42, Zermatt on 15 July), as the window chooses it: heading 148°, 35 mm.

*M1.17 stop 1 decisions (revisit freely):*

- **The look's choices are options first, defaults after the picks.** Each candidate is a display option on the same render, so a sheet
  shows them on one trace and stop 2 only changes a default.
- **The white balance is a camera's Kelvin, on the Planckian locus**, not "auto": a camera's daylight setting is a fixed Kelvin, and
  an automatic balance would take the gold out of golden hour. The EXR stays unbalanced.
- **The adaptation's threshold is the card's light with the sun about 2° up** (25 000 lux), so golden hour, the hero's light, is the
  meter's under any setting and only dusk changes.
- **The goldens are compared by means, not pixels**, with a tolerance measured to sit at four times a full re-roll of the noise, and by
  each channel of the PNG, so a white balance or a look change fails while a driver's rounding does not.
- **The LUT dispatches are sized, not split further**: the remaining 9.5 s is the GPU's 3.9 s doubled by the cap's pauses. Cutting it
  further means the cap off while the window waits (about 4–5 s), or a cheaper scattering integral that moves the sky a little; both are
  the user's call (asked at M1.17's end).

*M1.17 stop 1 gotchas:*

- **Windows PowerShell reads a script without a byte-order mark as ANSI**: `°` and `·` arrive as two characters each, and in
  `"sun $e° …"` the first of them joins the variable's name, so the label lost its number. Scripts with such characters carry a BOM
  (as `snow/render.ps1` does) and write `$($e)°`.
- **`"…" -f a, b, 100 * x` is `("…" -f a, b, 100) * x`** in PowerShell: the formatted string repeated x times, empty for a share
  under 1. Parenthesise arithmetic in a format's arguments.
- **`--labels` splits at commas**, labels and all ("spring dawn, skylight only" made eight labels for seven images).
- **A LUT builder's rate is not its cost per item at any size**: a builder with few, long items takes as long for a few thread groups as
  for all of them. A rate-sized dispatch needs a floor.
- **`--sun-elevation` takes the true height** (no refraction): the sidecar's `sun.elevationDegrees` is the refracted one (+0.4° at the
  horizon). The user's photos' heights were computed as true heights to match.
- **The meter makes dusk brighter than golden hour** (a mean of 132 at −1.9° against 60 at +2.7°): the card's light falls but the
  shadows go, so a metered twilight frame has no dark side.
